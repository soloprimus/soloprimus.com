Conviction Should Be a Hedge, Not Leverage

A sense of purpose can help you withstand uncertainty. But only if it rests on what you value, rather than on a promise that things will go your way.

At four in the morning, a strip of streetlight comes through the curtains and falls across the ceiling. A garbage truck reverses downstairs: a few beeps, a pause, then a few more. The pillow is damp. Your throat is dry. The phone by the bed is dark, a faint blue light along its edge. You're lying still, eyes open, while the numbers line themselves up: next month's mortgage, two payments due before the end of this month, an account balance that offers little comfort, a contract nobody has got back to you about. You add and subtract until the black outside turns gray and the first bus starts its engine.

Many of us have had a morning like that.

When I read Nietzsche's remark about having a reason to live, professional habit makes me reach for a balance sheet. I think of a life as having two sets of accounts. One records income, health, relationships, markets: things whose fluctuations we can't fully control. The other records what we are living for. We have some say in what goes into that account.

My question is whether inner conviction can offset external uncertainty. The answer, I suspect, depends on how closely the two move together. I want to look at the way people keep these accounts, and at the entries we get wrong.

1. The same night can weigh differently

The same uncertainty can weigh very differently on different people. It can even weigh differently on the same person at different times.

Think back through your own life. In some years a project collapsed, your income halved, and you still ate well, slept, and got up the next morning to carry on. In other years, a vaguely worded email or a payment arriving a week late was enough to keep you turning over until dawn. The size of the problem hadn't necessarily changed. Something in the person receiving it had.

In Twilight of the Idols, Nietzsche suggests that someone who has a why to live for can endure almost any how. The passage appears in aphorism 12 of “Maxims and Missiles”. Viktor Frankl later drew on it in Man's Search for Meaning, in his account of life in the concentration camps.

There is an obvious objection. Perhaps resilience is mostly temperament. Easygoing people sleep; anxious people don't. The “why” might be an explanation invented afterward by people who were going to sleep well anyway. I take that objection seriously. I've known people who could snore through the end of the world. Temperament matters.

But a relatively stable temperament cannot, by itself, account for every change within a person. Health, sleep, relationships, and practical burdens change too. Memory alone cannot rule those factors out. What interests me is whether, alongside them, the way we understand our circumstances changes the weight we feel.

The thoughts themselves often differ. In a year when you can cope, a setback makes you ask what to do next. Once you've worked it out, you can put it down. In a year when you can't, a problem of much the same size sets off a different question: “How have I ended up here again?” The first inquiry has an end. The second can work backward from a single email and put half a lifetime of disappointments on trial. What keeps you awake may be a hearing at which no verdict is ever delivered.

Why can the same person bear so much more in some years than in others?

2. Where the cost goes

Behavioral economics offers one way of thinking about this. In prospect theory, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky describe how people evaluate gains and losses relative to a reference point. A loss generally hurts more than an equivalent gain pleases.

Now borrow that idea for a life. If your reference point is “the secure life I should have had,” every canceled order, sick child, or falling market can register as something taken from you. If you have chosen a demanding path and expect it to include difficulty, some of those costs may fall into a different account. Borrowing Richard Thaler's idea of mental accounting, I think of it as a travel-expense account. This is my analogy, not a psychological category the theory has established.

Take a week of overtime. In one case, you are working on something you've decided matters. In another, you are repairing someone else's avoidable mistakes. Same hours, same cups of coffee, very different experience. In the first case, you may feel you are spending the hours; in the second, that the hours are being taken. One makes you want to continue. The other makes you want to leave.

Or consider writing and research. You spend three months working through dry source material, then discover that the original hypothesis doesn't hold. The draft goes in the bin. If your reference point is “three months of effort should produce a publication,” that is a loss. If it is “I want to understand this problem properly,” you have eliminated a wrong answer. The basket is still empty, but you may have learned why it couldn't hold water.

That sounds suspiciously like self-deception. Calling a loss a travel expense doesn't reduce the bill. Follow the argument far enough and every debt becomes tuition, with debtors cheerfully reporting for class. The novelist Wang Xiaobo would have enjoyed taking that absurdity apart.

The objection is half right. The bill hasn't changed. What may change is what you do next. If you don't abandon the work at the first painful setback, you remain able to finish it. Some outcomes depend less on who was cleverest in the hardest month than on who was still there afterward.

There must be a brake on this argument. A travel-expense account is not permission to stay forever. Continuing has to make sense on the basis of what lies ahead. Money and effort already spent are sunk costs; they cannot, on their own, justify spending more. One person asks whether the destination is still worth reaching. Another keeps going because of how far they've already come. The first question needs asking again and again. The second avoids the future altogether.

From the outside they can look identical: two people slogging down a muddy road. A useful test is to imagine starting today with the same money and time still available. Would you choose this path again? The person paying a worthwhile cost can say yes. The person trapped by sunk costs knows the answer is no, but can't bear to write off what has already gone. Mistaking that reluctance for conviction can be an expensive accounting error. You keep paying for a journey you no longer believe leads anywhere.

At the end of the month, both people still face the same number on the bill.

3. What are you certain about?

A hedge offsets a risk. Diversification depends on correlations, volatility, and the size of the positions involved; two assets need not move in opposite directions to offer a diversification benefit. But giving similar risks different names doesn't turn the combination into protection.

I think conviction works in something like this way. If it rests on what you want to spend your time doing and the person you want to become, it needn't rise and fall with every external result. Those commitments can survive a bad market. In that limited sense, they can act as a hedge.

If conviction rests on “I am certain to succeed” or “this industry can only go up,” it moves with the very outcome you need protection from. Good results reinforce it. Bad results destroy it. You've added leverage: when trouble comes, both positions fall together.

That is the difference between a prediction and a purpose. A prediction says what the world will do. A purpose says what you are willing to pursue even while the future remains unclear. There is a little distance between the second and the world's next verdict.

In investing, a particularly damaging loss can come from being forced to act at the worst possible moment. Someone without a considered reason for holding an asset has to reopen the entire question at every drawdown. A price decline becomes a judgment on the person who bought it. They may sell just to put an end to the hearing. Someone with a reason they have examined can begin with a narrower question: what has changed?

You see that escalation from price movement to self-judgment whenever markets become turbulent. If the original purchase came from a fashionable tip or faith in a rising line on a chart, a change in direction threatens the buyer's opinion of themselves. Liquidating the position offers relief from the doubt, even if the price has become exceptionally low. What they want to get rid of is the exam paper that keeps them awake.

If the purchase instead rests on reasoning they have checked, with an understanding of the expected return and the risks, they can revisit the fundamentals. A lower price alone need not force a decision. You wouldn't dismantle the roof in the heaviest rain merely to buy an umbrella.

The objection comes quickly: conviction also makes people stubborn. Some of history's most certain people were responsible for its greatest mistakes. There is no shortage of examples.

So the object of conviction matters. Values and direction can be held firmly while methods and conclusions remain open to evidence. A practical test is whether you can name what would make you change your mind. If you can, your position has a way of being tested. If you can't, you may have given stubbornness a more flattering name.

What part of my conviction could turn out to be wrong?

4. A market for counterfeit certainty

One day a post appeared in my feed: “This industry will win within three years. If you don't believe me, remember I said it.” The photograph showed an office with floor-to-ceiling windows. The comments were full of agreement. On the account's profile, the third post advertised a course. Limited time. Only a few places left.

There are plenty of people like this. Their certainty leaves no visible gap. There is usually an escape hatch somewhere, but it is reserved for the speaker. Certainty attracts attention, and attention can be converted into revenue. Demand creates supply. Soon the genuine article becomes difficult to distinguish from the imitation.

George Akerlof's market for lemons offers an analogy. A used-car seller knows more about the car than the buyer. If buyers can pay only for expected average quality, good cars may be withdrawn, worsening what remains. Something similar can happen with conviction. An outsider struggles to tell whether it has been earned. Genuine commitment can be costly; an emphatic performance is cheap. Enough of the latter makes everyone harder to trust.

Then certainty becomes something people buy to display. The purchase is a persona and a vocabulary: “I am the sort of person who knows where I am going.” Jean Baudrillard's idea of sign-value comes to mind, along with Thorstein Veblen's account of conspicuous consumption. The course earns its keep when the buyer announces it to friends.

There is a fair defense of pretending. Sometimes people grow into the posture. I've met people who began by acting confident and, in the process, managed to hold on.

Still, getting through a quiet period tells us little about what will happen in a storm. Actions that carry a real cost deserve more attention than statements. Speaking is cheap. Doing something can require payment.

That cost is central to the economics of signaling. When business is good, anyone can announce a long-term commitment. They may even be applauded for it. The harder test arrives when an industry turns down and cash runs short. One business quietly cancels research to dress up the next quarter while continuing to tell a ten-year story. Another cuts ceremony and overhead to preserve a project that may not produce anything for three years. The first statement is easy to imitate. The second is backed by forgone money and comfort.

Turn off the recording and look at where someone puts their last available resources. You may learn more about their priorities. Even then, an expensive commitment is no guarantee of a sound judgment.

At the next sharp market decline, we can watch who continues doing the work.

5. The margin you need to keep going

Conviction has a material limit. It is neither a luxury reserved for the comfortable nor a replacement for cash flow and health.

First comes the objection that people need food before meaning. Someone struggling to reach the next payday may have no attention left for a grand “why.” If you're hungry, finding a hot meal is the sensible place to begin. There is no argument against that.

Then there is survivorship bias. We hear the reasons given by people who made it through. Others had a sense of purpose and didn't survive the ordeal. They aren't here to tell the story. A why guarantees no ending.

My view is that meaning first changes how we bear a situation. Through our actions, it may also affect the outcome, but it cannot promise one. To continue the financial analogy, cash flow, health, and relationships provide the margin; conviction is the position we hold. The margin affects how long we can stay. The position affects what staying means. They need each other.

Each form of margin has a hard practical edge. Enough savings to cover a year's expenses can let you say no to an intolerable arrangement. That money buys the ability to protect a longer-term plan from a forced sale. If next month's rent depends on pleasing someone, even a good plan may have to be exchanged for groceries.

The body sets limits too. Serious illness or weeks without adequate sleep can exhaust a person's reserves. In acute physical pain, a dose of pain relief can matter more than the most lucid philosophy. And the one or two people who really know you may seem unremarkable until everything outside goes wrong. A hot meal and a conversation that isn't an inquiry into your success can keep one failure from becoming the whole of existence.

Without some support beneath it, a firm position can still be liquidated. I don't know how far this analogy travels. Readers will have to test its boundaries against their own experience.

Back to four in the morning. The strip of light is still there. So are the accounts. What is due will still fall due.

After waking, can you get back to sleep?
