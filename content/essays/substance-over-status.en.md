## A question for graduation season

Every graduation season brings a familiar set of questions: Should I join a big company or take a civil-service exam? Go to graduate school or join an investment bank? Stay in a major city or move back home? The details change, but the question underneath is often the same: Which path looks respectable, feels secure, and earns other people's approval?

Peter Thiel is a useful person to read against that instinct. He co-founded PayPal and founded Founders Fund. *Zero to One* grew out of an entrepreneurship course he taught at Stanford in 2012; his student Blake Masters turned his notes into the book. Thiel's own résumé looks like the model elite track: Stanford as an undergraduate, then Stanford Law. Yet he has spent years questioning the paths that credentials and convention make seem inevitable.

Two qualifications before I begin. "Substance" and "status" are my shorthand for themes in Thiel's work; he did not present a complete doctrine under those two labels. I'll mark my own extensions as I go. I've also relied on my recollection of some chapter references and interview details. Where I'm unsure, I've left the detail out. Check the book or original interview before quoting any of it.

## 1. Fierce competition may be a sign you chose the wrong contest

First, it helps to be precise about what Thiel means by competition. In the textbook model of perfect competition, companies earn only a normal return in long-run equilibrium; economic profit approaches zero. One of *Zero to One*'s central claims is that good companies tend to escape that condition, while those trapped in it spend much of their energy just staying alive. Around the book's publication, Thiel adapted one of its arguments for *The Wall Street Journal* under the title "Competition Is for Losers." The headline was sharper than the article.

I'm more interested in another point from chapter four, which draws on René Girard's theory of mimetic desire. We often learn what to want by watching other people. If everyone is competing for something, it starts to look valuable. The harder the rivalry, the more each side watches the other—and the easier it is to forget why the contest began. Thiel uses PayPal's early fight with X.com as an example. Both companies paid a high price for the rivalry; the contest ended only when they merged.

For a young person choosing a job, school, or title, the test is plain: Did you decide this prize was worth pursuing, or does it look worthwhile because everyone around you wants it? People often treat a crowded race as proof that the prize is valuable. Thiel's warning runs in the other direction: a crowd may mean everyone is reacting to the same signals.

That doesn't make competition bad in every setting. Exams, sports, and markets all use it to sort ability and spur improvement. As I read Thiel, his objection is to making "winning" the goal. If you can't say what the win leaves you with, it may be an empty victory.

## 2. A credential is a signal, not the thing itself

Thiel has spoken about failing to get a clerkship with the U.S. Supreme Court. My reading is that he was pointing to the pull of a prestigious job: much of its appeal may come from the fact that everyone wants it, not from whether the work suits you. Check the book for the exact details.

In a 2011 interview with TechCrunch's Sarah Lacy, he argued that American higher education had become a bubble. In broad terms, tuition kept rising as families came to believe that a student without an elite degree had no future. Education was being bought as insurance, but it was also a status race. Around the same period, Thiel launched the Thiel Fellowship, offering $100,000 each to young people who would leave school for two years to work on a project of their own.

The strongest part of this argument is the distinction between being certified and getting something done. In the classic economic model of signaling, a degree may help an employer judge someone whose productivity they don't yet know; the credential need not make that person more productive. An elite diploma answers, "How will other people read me?" Ability answers, "What can I make or do?" The two may be closely connected early in a career. Over time, what you've actually done matters more than where you studied.

There's a counterpoint worth keeping. The Thiel Fellowship is itself a form of elite selection. Many recipients were already unusually capable, so critics are right to ask whether it replaces one status system with another. An elite school offers more than a label, too: training, peers, and access to opportunities. Those benefits shouldn't be waved away. As I understand him, Thiel is questioning the impulse to treat the label as the goal, not the value of learning.

## 3. The "safe" path can hide a long-term bet

Chapter six of *Zero to One* sorts attitudes toward the future along two axes: pessimistic or optimistic, definite or indefinite. Thiel's target is "indefinite optimism": the belief that things will get better, without a concrete account of how or a plan to get there. In his view, finance, consulting, and law became default destinations for elite graduates partly because they preserve options. You can move in several directions later; you don't have to decide now what you want to build. The chapter's title, "You Are Not a Lottery Ticket," makes a related point: outcomes aren't just luck. You can plan and act to shape them.

There's an important distinction here. Keeping options open can be sensible, especially for someone carrying student debt or trying to get on stable footing. Thiel's narrower target is someone who has choices but keeps choosing not to choose because commitment feels uncomfortable.

Here I'm extending his argument. A "safe" path may protect your current income and standing while leaving your position ten years from now uncertain. If every job is chosen for where it might lead next, you can spend the years best suited to deep work accumulating a broadly acceptable résumé instead of a hard-to-replace ability. The risk is easy to miss because each individual year looks fine.

Imagine two people of the same age. One moves through three respectable jobs in five years. The other spends those five years working on the same narrow problem. The first has the more polished résumé. The second may have a judgment about that problem no one else can easily match. Which one is safer depends on whether you're looking at year one or year ten.

## 4. Substance begins with a truth others may reject

Thiel likes to ask candidates a question: What important truth do very few people agree with you on? It appears in the first chapter of *Zero to One*. He has also said it's hard to answer well. Most of our views come from school and the people around us; few are ideas we've worked out for ourselves.

He describes the shape of a good answer roughly this way: most people believe one thing; reality points the other way. What's easy to miss is that he wants a truth, not disagreement for its own sake. Contradicting the crowd just to look original is another way of following it.

Chapter eight, "Secrets," takes up a related idea: every good business is built around something others haven't noticed, whether it comes from nature or from people. Thiel pushes back against the belief that everything worth discovering has already been found and all that remains is to improve what's on the existing map.

That's where substance and status meet. Status is granted by other people and has to be seen. Substance is something you've figured out; it remains true even if no one has noticed yet. My own view is that if you can't name an independent judgment you've made in your field, much of your competitive edge may come from your title or platform. Change platforms, and that advantage may go with it.

One practical caution: an independent view still needs testing. Disagreeing with most people doesn't make you right. Start small. Write down a judgment about a specific question, the evidence behind it, and what would change your mind. Come back six months later and check.

## 5. Create value; don't just fight over a fixed share

Chapter three contrasts two figures from the book: in 2012, U.S. airlines earned an average of 37 cents per passenger, while Google's profit margin was about 21 percent. Thiel's explanation is that intense competition had pushed airline prices close to costs, while Google faced little competition in search, giving it room to set prices and invest over time.

His use of "monopoly" doesn't mean predatory conduct. He means a business has made something with no close substitute for the time being—the "creative monopoly" he describes in the book. His advice is to start in a small, specific market, become difficult to replace there, then expand. PayPal initially focused on power sellers on eBay.

Applied to a career, this is my extension: much of the anxiety at work comes from occupying a role with many substitutes, where you have to be more diligent or more visible than your colleagues just to hold your place. That's a fight over a fixed slice. A value-creation question is different: What use is my work to someone else? If I weren't here, could another person easily fill the gap?

Thiel's monopoly argument is contested. Antitrust authorities and many economists wouldn't accept monopoly as a general measure of success, and I have reservations too. Some monopoly profits come from creating something valuable; others come from using barriers to keep others out. Their consequences for social welfare are very different. What I take from Thiel is the method: find out whether what you make has a substitute before asking how to make it bigger.

## 6. Don't turn a life into a diversified index fund

Chapter seven is about power laws. In venture capital, the best investment in a successful fund can return more than all the others combined; Thiel draws on his own fund's experience. The implication is that a few decisions can determine most of the outcome, so the quality of a decision matters more than the number of decisions.

He carries the idea over to life: don't make your life a diversified index fund; find a few things worth pursuing deeply. I remember that extension appearing in the second half of the chapter. Check the book for the precise wording.

My reading is that a few choices matter especially much for a young person: which field to enter, whom to work with, and which problem to spend years on. Their consequences can differ enormously. By comparison, another internship or credential often adds little at the margin. In that sense, depth can matter more than breadth.

But there's a condition. A fund can make many bets and survive a few failures if it hits a handful of winners. Most people get only a few major bets in a lifetime, and a bad one can be hard to recover from. So don't copy the fund mechanically. Keep the cost of failure within what you can bear: earn enough to support yourself, then put some time and money into focused experiments. And remember that the stories we hear about people who bet right are selected survivors.

Thiel is speaking from a position of considerable resources. That belongs in the calculation when we read him.

## 7. Join a team without joining its status race

PayPal's early team is Thiel's main example in chapter ten, "The Mechanics of Mafia." He wanted each person to own a distinct responsibility and be judged on it. His reasoning was that many internal conflicts arise when responsibilities overlap: if two people do the same job, they can end up fighting over credit, resources, or importance.

The book doesn't make a separate argument about titles and rankings. What follows is my extension of his idea.

A title is cheap status currency inside a company. It costs no cash and satisfies the desire for recognition. When titles become the main concern, attention shifts from doing the work to being seen doing it. Clear responsibilities help move evaluation from "Who are you?" to "How well did you handle what you owned?" The second question is harder to perform for an audience.

I'd suggest two things when joining a team. Ask what you're responsible for and how that work will be judged; don't let "well-rounded development" become a substitute for a clear role. Then look at the relationship between promotion and value created. If advancement depends mostly on tenure or connections, build results in your own area that others can verify.

There are limits. Large companies need levels and reporting lines to coordinate and motivate people; they can't simply do away with hierarchy. The point here is where you put your own attention.

After reading Thiel, my overall impression is that he isn't arguing against hard work or elite schools. He's arguing against spending yourself on a goal you haven't examined, using other people's approval as the measure. His ideas also come from a privileged position and carry a survivor's perspective. They're worth considering, with that discount applied.

### Three questions to ask yourself

1. If no one could see or praise the position I'm pursuing, would I still spend the same time trying to get it?
2. Ten years from now, what will I have that no one can take away and few people can easily replace?
3. In my field, what do I believe that most of my peers don't? What evidence do I have?
